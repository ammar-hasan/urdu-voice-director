# Children and family dialogue

## Children

- Use short thought units and concrete vocabulary.
- Preserve curiosity, contradiction, and partial understanding.
- Let emotion change gradually: denial → concern → question → understanding.
- Keep repetitions that show urgency or active thinking.
- Avoid adult abstractions, polished moral summaries, and constant cuteness.
- Do not force baby talk. Age, personality, and language exposure matter more than a generic “child voice.”

Adult-sounding:

```text
میں اس اخلاقی اصول کے مضمرات سے اتفاق کرتا ہوں۔
```

Child-appropriate:

```text
اچھا... یعنی اکیلے میں بھی غلط کام، غلط ہی ہوتا ہے؟
```

## Parents and children

Warmth can coexist with a boundary:

```text
امی: [calm, firm] نہیں بیٹا، ابھی نہیں۔ پہلے کھانا کھا لو۔
```

Do not automatically add `بیٹا`, `بیٹی`, or an endearment to every parental line. Some families use names, nicknames, kinship terms, or no address term.

Preserve the family’s established `آپ`/`تم` pattern. In some households children are addressed with `آپ`; in others `تم` is warm and normal. Do not treat one household convention as universal.

## Siblings

Sibling talk can shift rapidly among teasing, alliance, rivalry, warning, and protection. Keep:

- quick overlaps;
- playful repetition;
- familiar particles;
- the difference between a warning meant to help and a threat meant to control.

Do not make every sibling say `یار` or insult each other.

## Teachers and students

Match the school culture and age:

- younger children often need a concrete prompt before an abstract explanation;
- respectful address may use `آپ`, `جی`, `سر`, `میڈم`, `استاد`, or a name/title pattern;
- a teacher can correct gently without becoming sentimental;
- a student’s `جی` may signal acknowledgment, uncertainty, compliance, or a request to continue.

## Educational and moral dialogue

Let the lesson arise from the situation. Prefer:

```text
اسی لیے ہمیں اکیلے میں بھی صحیح کام کرنا چاہیے۔
```

over a multi-sentence sermon that no child would naturally deliver. If the original lesson is longer, preserve its information while distributing it across age-appropriate turns.

## Evaluation prompts

Ask native listeners from the relevant age group or people who routinely speak with that age group:

- Would a child this age choose these words?
- Does the child understand too quickly?
- Is the adult warm without becoming patronizing?
- Does the exchange resemble family or classroom speech rather than translated instructional prose?
