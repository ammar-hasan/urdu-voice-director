# Natural spoken Urdu

Use this reference when a line is correct on paper but does not sound like something this speaker would say now.

## Contents

1. Thought units and particles
2. Sequencing, repetition, and fragments
3. Lexical identity and Roman Urdu
4. Read-aloud test

## Write thought units, not essay sentences

Spontaneous speech is planned incrementally. Let a speaker notice, react, qualify, and finish rather than delivering every premise in one polished sentence.

Written:

```text
چونکہ آپ نے مجھے پہلے اطلاع نہیں دی، اس لیے میرے لیے وہاں بروقت پہنچنا ممکن نہیں تھا۔
```

Natural, respectful:

```text
آپ نے مجھے پہلے بتایا ہی نہیں تھا۔ میں وقت پر کیسے پہنچتا؟
```

The rewrite must not erase a character who genuinely speaks formally. “Conversational” is not a license to flatten education, profession, period, or temperament.

## Preserve the work done by particles

Particles and response tokens often signal stance, recognition, contrast, turn-taking, or a changed understanding:

- `اچھا` — acknowledgment, transition, surprise, acceptance, or realization depending on contour and placement.
- `ارے` — noticing, mild surprise, correction, affectionate protest, or alarm.
- `اوہ` — realization, disappointment, sympathy, or a processing beat.
- `یعنی` — reformulation, checking an inference, or buying time.
- `تو` — contrast, consequence, shared premise, topic return, or emphasis.
- `ہی` — exclusivity or intensification; do not treat it as filler.
- `بھی` — addition, inclusion, concession, or emphasis.
- `نا` — request for alignment, insistence, reassurance, softening, or pressure; relationship-sensitive.
- `جی` — acknowledgment or respect; repetition and contour change its effect.
- `بس` — limit, dismissal, resolution, impatience, or reassurance.

Do not remove these mechanically, and do not sprinkle them into every line. Their prosodic and pragmatic value depends on context. Research supports special discourse/prosodic roles for markers such as `تو`, `ہی`, and `بھی`; the broader glosses above are editorial heuristics requiring scene judgment.

## Prefer native sequencing

Translated prose often preserves English information order:

```text
میں معذرت چاہتا ہوں کیونکہ میں آپ کو واپس کال کرنا بھول گیا۔
```

In an everyday exchange:

```text
معاف کیجیے گا، میں آپ کو واپس فون کرنا بھول گیا تھا۔
```

Or, among close friends:

```text
یار، معاف کرنا۔ میں واپس فون کرنا بھول گیا تھا۔
```

Do not insert `یار` unless the relationship supports it.

## Use repetition with a reason

Urdu conversation uses repetition naturally for urgency, disbelief, insistence, calming, and self-repair:

```text
رُکو، رُکو!
نہیں، میرا مطلب وہ نہیں تھا۔
اچھا، اچھا... سمجھ گیا۔
```

Keep purposeful repetition. Remove accidental redundancy that only comes from written padding.

## Let fragments remain fragments

Incomplete sentences may be the most natural form for interruption, reluctance, or realization:

```text
میں تو بس...
اگر امی کو پتا چل گیا تو—
نہیں، بات وہ نہیں ہے۔
```

Do not “repair” every fragment into a complete formal sentence.

## Protect lexical identity

- Keep ordinary borrowed words that are normal in the speaker’s community: `فون`, `میسج`, `کلاس`, `ہوم ورک`.
- Prefer a natural Urdu equivalent when the English term would sound introduced rather than lived.
- Preserve a literary, technical, rural, urban, regional, or period-specific lexicon when it defines the character.
- Do not make every speaker sound like a generic television child.

## Roman Urdu conversion

Infer intended words from the whole turn:

```text
acha tum ne us ko call ki thi?
```

Possible natural Urdu:

```text
اچھا، تم نے اسے فون کیا تھا؟
```

But `call` may need to remain `کال` in a workplace or code-switched scene. Ask only if the distinction matters.

## Read-aloud test

Read each turn at a normal conversational pace and check:

1. Can it be said in one comfortable breath where intended?
2. Does the emphasis fall naturally without explanation?
3. Is any clause present only because it looks elegant?
4. Could the listener follow the pronouns and omitted material?
5. Does it still sound like the same person?

Label the final judgment as editorial unless grounded in a cited grammar or corpus finding.
